## Executive read — what changed

Three findings from this research cycle materially change the Phase 0 plan.

**1. The Mauritanian used-vehicle age barrier appears to have been removed, and that reframes the competitive set.**
`VERIFIED` Mauritania imposed age limits in July 2014 — 5 years for passenger vehicles, 8 years for other categories. `VERIFIED` In October 2017 the Ministry of Finance issued a decree accepting imported vehicles **regardless of age**, substituting flat per-vehicle duty scales by horsepower for vehicles over 8 years old. `OPEN` Whether that regime is still current in 2026, and at what values after the 2018 MRO→MRU redenomination, is unverified and is the single highest-value customs question in the sprint.

The strategic consequence: a new Chinese SUV in Mauritania does not compete mainly against other new vehicles. It competes against an unrestricted flow of cheap aged imports. That raises the burden on the **service, warranty, parts, and residual-value proposition** — which is precisely the part of the model that is not yet built. It also strengthens the certified-used lane, because there is no regulatory wall keeping used stock out.

**2. Senegal just liberalised in the same direction, on a documented date.**
`VERIFIED` By decree of 24 October 2025, signed by President Bassirou Diomaye Faye, Senegal raised the import age limit from 8 years to **10 years** for passenger cars and light commercial vehicles under 3.5 tonnes, and to **15 years** for trucks and passenger-transport vehicles. The stated rationale was to reconcile economic, environmental, and social requirements and to fulfil a campaign commitment to the diaspora.

The strategic consequence: the Senegal hub thesis should be re-weighted **away from "sell newer vehicles into Senegal"** and **toward "own the service, parts, training, and quality-assurance layer for an ageing fleet that just got permission to age further."** That is a stronger position anyway, and it is defensible against Caetano's existing Jetour franchise rather than in conflict with it.

**3. The Senegal parts and workshop economy is large, fragmented, and already China-supplied — which is the corridor's real opening.**
`VERIFIED` Senegal imported USD 38.6 million of vehicle parts (HS 8708) in 2024, up from USD 28.1 million in 2022, with projected 6–8% annual growth to 2030. `VERIFIED` China is the **largest single source at 18% (USD 6.0 million)**, ahead of France at 16%. `VERIFIED` Over 2,000 active importers, roughly 8,500 shipments in 2024, and **no single company above 10% share**; CFAO Motors is the largest formal distributor at ~7%. `VERIFIED` Over 80% of vehicle imports are pre-owned and about 80% of registered vehicles are ten years or older.

The strategic consequence: there is no incumbent to displace in parts. A governed, provenance-tracked parts and service layer is the least contested, highest-frequency, most local-ownership-compatible entry point in the entire corridor — and it is more defensible than vehicle distribution, where OEM authorization is not held.

## Q1 — Mauritanian vehicle market: registrations, imports, age, powertrain, buyer type

### What is verified

`VERIFIED` **Macroeconomic frame (World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, 2026 edition):** real GDP growth 6.8% (2023), 6.3% (2024), 4.2% (2025 estimate), forecast 4.4% (2026), 4.5% (2027), 4.8% (2028). CPI inflation 5.0% (2023), 2.5% (2024), 1.6% (2025), forecast 4.8% (2026). Population 5.3 million (2025). Poverty at the USD 4.20/day line 25.2% (2025). Current account −5.8% of GDP (2025), forecast −6.2% (2026). The ouguiya depreciated only 0.13% year-on-year against the USD in 2025. Greater Tortue Ahmeyim (GTA) gas exports began in 2025.

`VERIFIED` **Demand-adjacent industrial pipeline (U.S. ITA Country Commercial Guide):** SNIM is executing a rail and port modernization program to **double export capacity by 2030**. Kinross Tasiast has reached record output at **24,000 tonnes per day**. Boulenouar wind farm is operating with World Bank financing of a first battery storage project. Green hydrogen projects "Nour" (Chariot/TotalEnergies) and "AMAN" (CWP) are in development. Company registration through the APIM one-stop shop now takes **one to two days**.

### What is NOT verified — and this matters

`OPEN` **There is no reliable public dataset for Mauritanian vehicle registrations by make, model, age, powertrain, geography, or buyer type.** Aggregator sites (CEIC, TrendEconomy, GoodCarBadCar, TheGlobalEconomy) surface partial or modelled series; none constitutes a registration record. UN Comtrade / OEC mirror-trade data for HS 8703 is obtainable but reports **customs value by origin, not units, condition, or buyer**.

**This is the defining data gap of the whole program.** Every volume, price, and mix assumption downstream inherits it.

## Q2 — Landed and road-ready cost under current Mauritanian rules

### The tax stack as currently evidenced

| Component | Rate | Basis | Label | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MFN customs duty — top band | 20% | CIF | `VERIFIED` | WTO TPR 2018 (bands 0/5/13/20; 20% on ~39% of lines, mainly final consumer goods) |
| MFN duty — simple average, all lines | 12.1% (2017) | CIF | `VERIFIED` | WTO TPR 2018 |
| MFN duty — weighted mean applied | 8.68% | CIF | `VERIFIED` | Lloyds Bank Trade country profile |
| VAT | 16% | CIF + duty | `VERIFIED` | Mauritania Trade Portal; WTO TPR |
| Statistical fee | 1% **or** 3% | CIF | `CONFLICT` | Mauritania Trade Portal says 1% on 90%+ of tariff lines; Lloyds says 3% on the majority of imports |
| Culture/Sport tax (PCS) | 1% | CIF, HS chapters 21–98 | `VERIFIED` | Mauritania Trade Portal |
| Minimum flat-rate tax (IMF) | 2.5% of CIF **or** 2% on exports | CIF | `CONFLICT` | Mauritania Trade Portal says 2.5% of CIF customs value on imports; WTO TPR describes IMF at 2% in an export context |
| Declaration processing fee | 3,000 old ouguiya (≈ USD 8.60) | Per declaration | `VERIFIED` | Mauritania Trade Portal |
| Pre-shipment inspection | SGS control | Imports above USD 5,000 | `VERIFIED` | Lloyds Bank Trade |

`VERIFIED` **Customs valuation for used vehicles does not use transaction value.** Mauritania applies a *minimum importation value* to used cars — the ITA has separately reported Argus-based minimum values. This means **the invoice price of a used vehicle does not determine its taxable base**, and a landed-cost model built on invoice value will be wrong for the certified-used lane.

`VERIFIED` **Age regime.** 2014: 5 years (passenger) / 8 years (other). October 2017: age limit removed; vehicles over 8 years old assessed at flat duty scales by fiscal horsepower, quoted at the time as 512,000 old UM (2–6 HP), and 850,000–1,420,000 old UM at higher horsepower. Customs had already been clearing at 1,200,000 old UM from 2016 as a de facto relaxation.

`OPEN` **Currency basis.** Those figures are in pre-2018 ouguiya (MRO). Mauritania redenominated MRO→MRU at 10:1 in January 2018. Any current schedule must be re-obtained in MRU. **Do not convert these historical figures forward.**

`OPEN` **Road-ready costs are entirely unquantified:** port handling and demurrage at Nouakchott, customs broker fees, inland transport, first registration and plates, technical inspection, compulsory third-party insurance, and any first-service or PDI cost.

### What this means for the model

`ASSUMPTION` For planning only, the model in `models/` uses an indicative new-vehicle stack of **20% duty + 1% statistical + 1% PCS + 2.5% IMF on CIF, then 16% VAT on (CIF + duty)**, producing an effective uplift on CIF of roughly **45–47%** before port, broker, transport, registration, and insurance. Adding a planning allowance for those road-ready items takes the total uplift to an indicative **52–58% over CIF**.

**This number is a planning placeholder and must not be published, quoted to a buyer, or used to set a price.** `OPEN` — validation owner Diallo, next action: obtain a written landed-cost simulation from a licensed Mauritanian customs broker for three specific HS lines (8703.23 petrol SUV, 8703.60 hybrid, 8704 pickup), new and used, with all fees itemised.

## Q3 — What actually drives purchase decisions, by segment

`VERIFIED` **Continental attitudinal baseline (Sagaci Research, 29-country survey, December 2025):** car ownership 17% across surveyed countries. **75% of African car purchases are used vehicles.** Current fuel mix: petrol 76%, diesel 17%, hybrid 4%, electric 3%. **Next-purchase intent: hybrid 34%, petrol 27%, diesel 13%, electric 12%.** On Chinese brands: **54% would probably or definitely consider one; 17% would not; roughly 30% report unfamiliarity.**

Two readings matter. First, hybrid intent already exceeds petrol intent — which supports the sourcebook's petrol-and-hybrid-first sequencing over an EV-led portfolio, and argues for putting hybrids in the shortlist rather than treating them as a later phase. Second, the binding constraint on Chinese brands is **unfamiliarity, not rejection** — a marketing and demonstration problem, not a preference problem. That is fixable with a demonstration vehicle and a visible workshop; it is not fixable with a lower price.

`ASSUMPTION` Segment hypotheses remain as stated in the market baseline v0.1 and are **not** upgraded by this research. They require the 25 buyer interviews in the evidence sprint.

`OPEN` No Mauritania-specific segment research exists. The Sagaci survey's country composition should be checked for whether Mauritania is included; if it is not, the figures are regional context, not local evidence. Validation owner: program team.

### The attribute that decides this market

`ASSUMPTION` — but with strong support from the transcript analysis and from the age-limit finding: **residual value is the product.** In a market with unrestricted aged imports and deep Toyota parts familiarity, a buyer purchasing an unfamiliar Chinese SUV is underwriting a bet that the vehicle will be serviceable and saleable in five years. The corridor's differentiated offer is not the vehicle — it is a documented VIN service history, a parts guarantee, and a stated buy-back or trade-in reference. That is a HAMAL product, not a supplier product.

## Q4 — Chinese brands in Mauritania and neighbouring markets

`VERIFIED` **Chinese brands active in African ownership data:** Chery, BYD, Haval (Great Wall Motors), Jetour, JAC Motors, and Omoda & Jaecoo (Sagaci, Dec 2025). None yet appears in top market-share rankings continentally.

`VERIFIED` **Jetour is already established in Senegal** through Salvador Caetano's Africa operation, trading publicly as "Jetour Sénégal – Caetano." This confirms the constraint identified in the strategy transcript: **Senegal is not a blank slate, and no "exclusive West Africa distributor" language can be used.**

`REPORTED` **Chery acquired Nissan's South African manufacturing plant** (multiple wire reports, August 2026), described as part of a shift in EV production toward new markets. `OPEN` Plant capacity, models, production start date, and any West African distribution implications are unverified — the primary sources fetched returned partial or errored content. Validation owner: program team; next action: obtain Chery/Nissan official statements and South African government confirmation before this is used in any briefing.

`OPEN` **Chinese-brand presence in Mauritania specifically is unmapped.** No dealer, service point, parts inventory, warranty term, or resale data has been obtained. This is evidence-sprint question 13 and remains unanswered.

### Strategic reading

The Caetano finding should be treated as an **asset, not an obstacle**. The corridor's approach to Dakar is a cross-border service and warranty conversation — will Caetano honour service on a Mauritanian-sold vehicle, and will they wholesale genuine parts — not a territorial one. `PROPOSAL` A written parts-supply and cross-border service understanding with an existing franchise holder is worth more in Phase 0 than an OEM appointment the program cannot yet support.

## Q5 — Workshop, technician, and vocational capacity

`VERIFIED` **Senegal (Firmus Advisory, 2024–25 data):** parts imports USD 38.6M (2024) vs USD 28.1M (2022); 6–8% projected annual growth 2025–2030; **over 2,000 active parts importers**; ~8,500 shipments recorded in 2024; **no single firm above 10% share**, CFAO largest formal distributor at ~7%. Import sources: **China 18% (USD 6.0M)**, France 16% (USD 5.5M), Spain 10% (USD 3.4M, fastest growing), UAE 9% (re-export hub). Informal clusters concentrated in Dakar's **Colobane** district, functioning as a re-export node for **Mali, The Gambia, and Guinea**. Fleet: **over 80% of vehicle imports pre-owned; ~80% of registered vehicles ten years or older**; average fleet age over 10 years.

`CONFLICT` The same source cites Senegalese registration age restrictions of **6 years (passenger) and 8 years (commercial), partially enforced** — which does not reconcile with the October 2025 decree setting 10 and 15 years. Most likely explanation is that the source predates the decree or describes a distinct registration rule rather than the import rule. **Resolution required before either figure is used externally.** Validation owner: program team; next action: obtain the decree text from the Journal Officiel.

`OPEN` **No count exists of workshops, technicians, auto-electricians, diagnostic specialists, vocational institutions, or parts sellers in either Mauritania or Senegal**, and therefore no defensible estimate of what proportion could be upskilled to modern Chinese petrol, hybrid, or EV platforms. The Firmus source explicitly notes rapid growth of small repair workshops in Dakar but provides no count.

`PROPOSAL` Because no register exists, **the corridor should build one.** A workshop and technician registry — geolocated, capability-graded, tool-audited, and consent-based — is (a) the direct answer to evidence-sprint questions 15 and 16, (b) a HAMAL module already specified in the sourcebook, (c) a genuine local data asset that stays in Africa, and (d) the credential that makes any OEM conversation serious. This is the corridor's most under-priced strategic asset and the recommended first build.

## Q6 — Entity and licensing architecture

Full treatment is in `docs/00-governance/ENTITY-AND-CAPITAL-ARCHITECTURE-MEMO.md`. Summary of the research inputs:

`VERIFIED` Mauritania operates an investment promotion agency (APIM) as a one-stop shop, with company registration in **one to two days** (U.S. ITA).
`VERIFIED` Importers must enrol in the **Central Importers Register**; a tax identification number (NIF) is required for importers above a value threshold; declarations run through **ASYCUDA World**; Ministry of Trade import authorization is required in principle though not enforced at clearance.
`OPEN` Mauritania's current Investment Code text and foreign-ownership provisions could not be retrieved (UNCTAD Investment Laws Navigator returned 403). Validation owner: program team; next action: obtain the code through APIM or Mauritanian counsel.
`OPEN` Whether Mauritania is an OHADA member state materially affects company forms, security interests, and insolvency treatment and **must be confirmed by counsel, not inferred**. Senegal is an OHADA and UEMOA/BCEAO member; **Mauritania is neither in UEMOA nor in the CFA franc zone** — it uses the ouguiya (MRU) and its own central bank. Any cross-border corridor structure therefore spans **two currencies, two banking regulators, and two legal traditions**. This is a first-order structuring fact, not a detail.

## Q7 — Compliant local and diaspora investor participation

`OPEN` — and this is the question where the program is most exposed to doing something inadvertently unlawful.

Research established the structural constraint rather than the answer:

- Mauritania and Senegal sit in **different monetary and regulatory zones** (MRU / Banque Centrale de Mauritanie vs XOF / BCEAO and the UEMOA regional securities regulator CREPMF). A single instrument offered across both is two regulatory problems, not one.
- Diaspora participation adds the **investor's** jurisdiction — commonly France, Spain, the US, and the Gulf — each with its own securities, KYC/AML, and cross-border solicitation rules. A public web page inviting investment is capable of constituting a solicitation in jurisdictions the program has no licence in.
- Customer **preorders and deposits** are a separate exposure from investment: they raise consumer-protection, refund, delivery-failure, and currency-movement obligations, and in some framings can be characterised as taking deposits from the public.

`PROPOSAL` **Sequence the capital question behind the commercial one.** Until one full commercial loop is proven — vehicle landed, sold, serviced, part supplied, warranty honoured, cash collected — there is nothing that can be responsibly offered to anyone. The correct Phase 0 posture is: **no deposits, no investor solicitation, no returns language, no public investment page.** The evidence sprint and the first pilot are funded by principal capital or a single accredited relationship under counsel, or they are not funded.

`OPEN` Validation owner: Major, with counsel in Mauritania, Senegal, and the primary diaspora jurisdiction. Next action: scoping opinion on (a) whether an SPV interest in a taxi fleet constitutes a security in each jurisdiction, (b) what a lawful preorder deposit structure requires, (c) whether Islamic-finance structures (murabaha, ijara) can be sourced from a **licensed local institution** rather than replicated internally — which is strongly preferred, because it moves licensing risk to a regulated counterparty.

## Q8 — Unit economics and risk allocation

`VERIFIED` **Drive-to-own benchmark — Moove (Nigeria/Ghana), as reported by Techpoint Africa and Rest of World:** terms of **18–48 months**, **zero down payment**, ~5,000 vehicles purchased in 2022. Original driver obligation: 12 hours/day, six days/week, remitting **₦9,400/week (~USD 20)**. Revised after driver protests to 10 trips over 8.5 hours, remitting **₦6,500/week (~USD 14)** if the target is met, ₦9,400 if not. Documented failure modes: **repossession of vehicles from drivers missing KPIs**; maintenance delays; promised health insurance inaccessible in practice; aggressive collection; elimination of leave; and external shocks (fuel and cash scarcity, Dec 2022–Mar 2023) breaking payment capacity. Structural vulnerability explicitly noted: **large upfront capital committed per vehicle, recovered slowly.**

This is the single most instructive precedent in the dossier, and it should be read as a warning, not a template. Moove's model concentrates **asset risk, credit risk, utilisation risk, maintenance risk, and reputational risk in one balance sheet** and pushes downside onto drivers through KPI-linked payments. Every criticism that the sourcebook's anti-extraction test is designed to catch — who bears risk, who receives upside, does training lead to ownership or dependency — Moove fails at least partly.

`PROPOSAL` **Design the driver-to-owner ladder so that a bad month does not cost a driver the asset.** Concretely: payment obligations sized against evidenced local net earnings rather than against the financier's recovery schedule; a maintenance obligation carried by the fleet SPV rather than the driver; a documented cure period before repossession; and equity that **accrues and is retained** on partial performance rather than resetting. If those cost too much, the taxi vertical is not yet viable — which is a legitimate finding.

`OPEN` **No Mauritanian or Senegalese taxi economics have been verified**: daily gross revenue, fuel cost, fare regulation, licence cost and transferability, insurance, typical vehicle price, utilisation, and driver net income are all unmeasured. The model in `models/` uses labelled `ASSUMPTION` inputs for all of these, structured so each can be replaced with a measured figure. Validation owner: Diallo (Nouakchott) and a named Senegal counterpart (Dakar); next action: 10 driver interviews per city capturing a full day's cash flow.

## Q10 — Anti-extraction governance and technology transfer

The sourcebook's ten-question anti-extraction test is sound. Research suggests four mechanisms that make it **measurable** rather than aspirational:

`PROPOSAL` **1. Data residency and ownership as a contractual term, not a value statement.** The workshop registry, VIN service histories, customer records, and fleet telematics are the corridor's durable asset. The country operating company should own them, HAMAL should process them under licence, and no supplier, OEM, or investor should receive raw customer or service data as a condition of participation. Write it into the first supplier agreement, before there is leverage to lose.

`PROPOSAL` **2. Local-content measured as margin capture, not headcount.** Track the percentage of gross margin per vehicle and per service event that is booked in the country operating company versus offshore. Headcount can rise while margin leaves; margin share cannot be gamed as easily.

`PROPOSAL` **3. The ownership ladder needs a defined instrument at each rung.** "Learn → certify → perform → generate revenue → manage assets → earn ownership → train others" is currently a sequence without legal content. Each transition needs: a named qualifying event, the instrument granted (profit share, phantom equity, real equity, asset title), the vesting condition, and the forfeiture rule. Without that, it is a motivational diagram.

`PROPOSAL` **4. A published knowledge-retention test.** After any Chinese technical specialist rotation ends, measure what remains: number of locally certified technicians, diagnostics performed without remote assistance, first-time-fix rate, and whether a local trainer can certify the next cohort unaided. If a foreign specialist's departure degrades capability, transfer did not occur.

`ASSUMPTION` These four mechanisms are program design proposals, not verified best practice, and should be reviewed against comparable technology-transfer programs before adoption.
